# Drafting rules

These are the rules TrendJack's own drafter follows. Use them when you write a draft or an alternative in chat, so that a chat draft reads like a TrendJack draft.

## Order of authority

Build the draft from these parts, in this order. A later part outranks an earlier one:

1. The opening line: "You write as [company name], reacting to news the same day it breaks." For an executive voice: "You write as [name], [role] at [company name], reacting to news the same day it breaks."
2. The format's job and rules, below.
3. The voice guide from `get_company_profile`.
4. The active content pillars from `get_company_profile`.
5. The machine-writing rules, below.
6. The company's language rules from `get_company_profile`. These come from the company itself. They override everything above, including your own sense of what reads well.

Then apply these two rules:

- Write in this company's voice, not in the voice of a brand account in general. For an executive voice, write in the first person as the executive. Nothing says how that person writes, so do not invent a personal style.
- Ground the draft in the signal's suggested angle. If the angle does not hold up against the story, write the closest position that does. Do not invent facts that are not in the signal.

## Format rules

### LinkedIn post (`linkedin`)

Job: a short LinkedIn post reacting to news the same day it breaks. A good one takes a single clear position and defends it in a few lines. It does not summarise the news. The reader can get the headline anywhere.

- Open with the line a reader stops scrolling for. No rhetorical questions, no "Let that sink in".
- Write two to five short paragraphs after it, one idea each.
- No hashtags, no emoji, no call to action, no sign-off.
- Do not hedge. State one opinion.

### X post (`x`)

Job: one post for X reacting to news the same day it breaks. One post, not a thread, and not the first post of a thread. The whole post must be under 280 characters, the hook and the body counted together.

- Say the sharpest version of the position and stop. On X, brevity is the argument.
- Hard limit: 280 characters in total. If the point needs more, cut the point down. Do not continue into a second post.
- No hashtags. No thread markers such as "1/" or "a thread:".
- An emoji is allowed, but it must earn the character it costs. Usually it does not.
- No call to action, no link. The post stands alone.

### Thought leadership (`long_form`)

Job: a blog post or newsletter essay built out of the news rather than reporting it. This format has room to argue: state the position, show why the obvious reading is wrong, and take the strongest objection seriously before you answer it.

- Give it a headline that states the position, not a label for the topic.
- Write 15 to 40 short paragraphs. Each one is a single move in the argument.
- Use the news as evidence, not as the subject. The reader knows what happened.
- Prefer the concrete: name the thing, say the number, describe the specific case.
- No headings, no bullet lists, no sign-off, no hashtags, no emoji.
- Earn the length. A long piece that could have been a short post is a worse post.

### Email to a prospect (`email_prospect`)

Job: a cold email to a prospect that uses this news as the reason it lands today. It is a real email: it has an addressee, it asks for something specific, and it has a sign-off.

- Address a role, not a person. No contact record exists. Open with this placeholder exactly: Hi [first name],
- In the first two lines, say why this news matters to their business in particular, not to the market in general.
- Make one named, concrete ask: a twenty-minute call this week, an introduction to the owner of the problem, or a look at one of their numbers. Not "let me know if this resonates".
- Write six to ten short paragraphs, greeting and sign-off included. Shorter is better.
- Sign it off with a plain sign-off and this placeholder exactly: [your name]
- No hashtags, no emoji, no marketing superlatives, no fake familiarity.
- Write a subject line that says the thing. Not a teaser, not a label, and never the company's own name alone.

### Pitch to a journalist (`email_journalist`)

Job: a pitch to a journalist off the back of this news. A journalist owes the sender nothing and reads a hundred pitches a week. In the first three lines, the email must make clear why this story, why now, and why this company deserves a quote in it.

- Address a role, not a person. No contact record exists. Open with this placeholder exactly: Hi [first name],
- Why now: name the story that just broke, and say what makes it live today.
- Why them: name the beat or the angle the journalist covers. Use a placeholder the sender fills in if the signal does not tell you.
- The offer: a comment on the record, a named person available today, and one line of what that person would say. That line is the pitch.
- Write five to nine short paragraphs, greeting and sign-off included.
- Sign it off with a plain sign-off and this placeholder exactly: [your name]
- No embargoes, no attachments, no press-release register, no hashtags, no emoji. Do not claim exclusivity you were not given.
- The subject line is the headline you offer the journalist. Write it as one.

### Media comment (`media_comment`, chat only)

Job: a quotable statement that a PR team can put straight into a journalist's article, attributed to the named executive. It is not an opinion post. It is the one thing worth quoting, said the way a person says it out loud.

- Open with the sentence a journalist would pull as the quote on its own.
- Write one to three short paragraphs in total, opening line included. State the position. The reporter already has the story.
- Write it as something a named person said, ready to attribute as it is. No headline, no hashtags, no emoji, no call to action, no sign-off.
- Do not hedge. State the position as a fact the executive will put their name to.

## Machine-writing rules

A draft that breaks one of these rules reads as generated, whatever it says.

- Never write "not X but Y". That covers "not just", "not only", "not merely", "it is not X, it is Y", and the same contrast split over two sentences. Say the point once, straight.
- Never close on a line that restates the line before it. No "that is the real win", no "read that again", no one-word paragraph carrying weight it did not earn. Stop on the last real fact.
- Never dress an ordinary point as a hidden truth. No "the real question", no "at its core", no "in reality", no "what really matters", and no "X is the Y of Z" aphorism.
- Never announce the point before you make it. No "here is the thing", no "here is what you need to know", no "let me be clear", and no staged "honestly?" before a routine claim.
- Never argue with someone who is not there. Do not raise an objection nobody made, and do not reject an option nobody proposed.
- Count the parts of a list before you write it. Three items are right only when the thing has three parts. Two or four are fine.
- Use no em dash and no en dash. A comma, a full stop, a colon or a bracket says how two clauses relate.
- Name the actor and keep the verb active. Write "we held the price", not "the price was held".
- Use is, are and has. Not "serves as", "stands as", "represents a", "boasts".
- State the fact and drop its significance. Nothing is pivotal, a testament, a turning point, a step in the right direction, or a sign that the future is bright.
- Say how two things connect. Not "linked to", not "tied to", not "associated with".
- Never sound like an advertisement or a press release.
- Never use these words: actually, additionally, align with, boasts, bolster, breathtaking, commitment to, crucial, deep dive, delve, diverse array, emphasising, enduring, enhance, exemplifies, featuring, foster, garner, gated, groundbreaking, highlight, in the heart of, interplay, intricate, key, landscape, meticulous, must-visit, natural beauty, nestled, pivotal, profound, quietly, renowned, rich, robust, showcase, stunning, tapestry, testament, underscore, valuable, vibrant.
- Write plain text in the draft itself. No markdown, no bold, no headings, no bullet lists, no arrows, no title case.
- Use straight quotation marks.
- Carry nothing over from the conversation into the draft. No greeting, no praise, no offer to expand, and no note about what you could not find.
- Vary the sentence length. Real writing runs short, then long, then short again.
- Invent nothing. Where the signal gives you no fact, name, number or quote, write the sentence without one.

## Where the parts go

TrendJack stores a draft in three parts:

- `hook`: the opening line. For the two email formats, `update_draft_text` takes the subject line in `hook`.
- `subject`: the subject line. Email formats only.
- `body`: the rest of the draft, as plain prose. A blank line is a paragraph break.
